At Red Dragons we teach the Shaolin curriculum as it was preserved and passed down from the Shaolin Temple (少林寺) in Henan, China. Every form, every weapon, every set has survived because the physics inside it is correct. Nothing is decorative — every movement solves a problem.

Below you will find the named forms and sets that make up our curriculum, with their Chinese names, pronunciations, and an explanation of what each one teaches. Beginners start at the top; mastery takes a lifetime.

基础概念

What is Tao Lu? 套路

套路 (tào lù) — literally "set road" or "linked forms" — are the formal sequences of techniques that are the backbone of Shaolin training. A tao lu is not a dance; it is a physics lecture delivered through the body.

Each tao lu encodes:

  • Attack and defence combinations against specific threats
  • Footwork patterns that train weight transfer and rooting
  • Breath timing that builds internal pressure (qi)
  • Transitions that train explosive acceleration and deceleration

Solo practice of tao lu builds the body's structural vocabulary. Partner drilling then applies that vocabulary under pressure. The Shaolin monks understood that reliable technique must be grooved — written into the nervous system through thousands of correct repetitions before it can be called upon without thought.

This is why we say: 它都是物理学 — it's all physics. Repetition is just the process of making the physics automatic.

「拳打千遍，其理自现」

Practice a form a thousand times and its principles reveal themselves.

空手套路

Bare-Hand Forms 拳法

Shaolin bare-hand forms (拳法 quánfǎ) are trained before weapons. You must first understand what your own body can do. These are the core empty-hand tao lu of our curriculum, taught in order of complexity.

初级
少林小洪拳 Shàolín Xiǎo Hóng Quán

Small Red Fist

The entry tao lu for all beginners. "Small" refers not to its importance but to the compact, tight structure of its techniques. Xiao Hong Quan establishes the foundational stances — horse stance (马步), bow stance (弓步), empty stance (虚步) — and introduces the student to the direct relationship between stable base and explosive strike. Every intermediate and advanced form builds on what Xiao Hong Quan teaches.

StancesBasic StrikesBlocksFootwork
初级
少林大洪拳 Shàolín Dà Hóng Quán

Large Red Fist

The natural progression from Small Red Fist. Da Hong Quan expands the range of motion, introduces longer stepping patterns and more complex combination sequences. Where Xiao Hong Quan is tight and upright, Da Hong Quan develops dynamic power through larger mechanical levers — longer steps, wider rotation, deeper stances. Students begin to feel the difference between muscular effort and structural efficiency.

Power GenerationCombinationsDynamic Stepping
初级
少林七星拳 Shàolín Qīxīng Quán

Seven Star Fist

Named after the seven stars of the Big Dipper (北斗七星), this form maps seven primary attacking and defensive points on the body to seven corresponding techniques. It is one of the most systematic forms in the Shaolin syllabus — a structured vocabulary lesson that ensures students can cover all high-threat angles. The pattern of the footwork traces the shape of the constellation.

AnglesTarget PointsPattern Work
中级
少林罗汉拳 Shàolín Luóhàn Quán

Arhat Fist

One of the oldest forms in the Shaolin canon, attributed to the original monk-warriors who were inspired by the postures of the Luohan (Arhat) statues in the temple. Luohan Quan is characterised by sudden changes of rhythm — explosive bursts followed by held stillness — which trains the student in the critical skill of start/stop power. The pauses are not rest; they are isometric loading for the next release.

Explosive PowerRhythm ChangeIsometric Training
中级
少林炮拳 Shàolín Pào Quán

Cannon Fist

Pao Quan is the form of linear, direct, overwhelming force. Named for the cannon — a weapon that does not deflect, redirect, or finesse; it simply projects maximum force in a straight line. This form develops the ability to generate concentrated striking power through correct alignment of ankle, knee, hip, shoulder and fist. The physics lesson: a chain transfers force only as well as its weakest link. Pao Quan forges all the links.

Linear PowerWhole-Body AlignmentStriking
中级
少林通背拳 Shàolín Tōngbèi Quán

Through-Back Fist

Tong Bei — "through the back" — teaches the student to originate striking force from the centre of the back rather than the shoulder or arm. The back muscles are the largest and most powerful in the upper body; this form is the key that unlocks them. Techniques appear whip-like and long-range. Training Tong Bei fundamentally changes how a student understands upper-body mechanics — the arm becomes a transmission, not the engine.

Back PowerLong-Range StrikingWhip Mechanics
高级
少林心意把 Shàolín Xīnyì Bǎ

Mind-Intent Form

Considered one of the deepest forms in the Shaolin system, Xinyi Ba bridges external and internal practice. Where beginners use visible physical effort, Xinyi Ba teaches that intention (意 yì) directs force before the body moves. This is not mysticism — it is motor neuroscience: the nervous system fires before the muscle contracts, and training that pre-activation pathway makes movement faster than conscious thought. This form is the beginning of true internal Shaolin practice.

Internal PowerIntent TrainingAdvanced
高级
少林地趟拳 Shàolín Dìtǎng Quán

Ground Tumbling Fist

Ditang Quan takes the fight to the ground — not in the grappling sense, but through sweeping low kicks, rolls, and techniques performed close to or on the floor. It teaches evasion through lowering the centre of gravity rather than retreating, and attacks from angles that upright fighting cannot reach. Physically demanding; requires strong hip flexibility, coordination, and core control. A reminder that the ground is not a failure state — it is a tactical position.

Ground WorkLow KicksEvasionAcrobatics

五形拳

Five Animal Fist 五形拳

The Five Animal system (五形拳 Wǔ Xíng Quán) is perhaps the most famous element of Shaolin martial arts. Each animal represents a distinct strategy, body quality, and physics principle. A complete martial artist trains all five and learns to move fluidly between them — because opponents do not fight in a single mode.

龙

Dragon — 龙形

Lóng Xíng

Core quality: Spirit, flexibility, whole-body integration

The Dragon does not use brute force — it flows, spirals, and coils. Dragon techniques train the spine as a weapon: every technique originates from a spiral through the vertebrae. This develops core strength that is dynamic rather than static, and teaches the student to move as one unit rather than a collection of independent limbs. Dragon is the spirit that gives the other four animals life.

  • Spinal rotation and whipping power
  • Whole-body coordination
  • Spirit and unpredictability
虎

Tiger — 虎形

Hǔ Xíng

Core quality: Bone strength, explosive force, ferocity

Tiger trains the bones and tendons. Tiger techniques are powerful, direct, and devastating — clawing strikes, pouncing footwork, bone-crushing grips. The physics principle is mass and momentum: the Tiger gets its whole weight behind every technique. Tiger practice develops dense, structural strength and the mental ferocity to commit fully without hesitation.

  • Bone and tendon conditioning
  • Claw strikes and grabs
  • Explosive forward momentum
豹

Leopard — 豹形

Bào Xíng

Core quality: Speed, penetrating force, muscle power

Where the Tiger uses mass, the Leopard uses speed. Leopard Fist (豹拳) strikes with the second knuckles rather than the full fist — a smaller contact surface concentrating the same force into a fraction of the area, dramatically increasing pressure per square centimetre. Leopard teaches acceleration: from stillness to maximum velocity in minimal time. It develops fast-twitch muscle fibres and the ability to deliver multiple strikes in a single breath.

  • Leopard knuckle strikes
  • Explosive acceleration
  • Rapid combination attacks
蛇

Snake — 蛇形

Shé Xíng

Core quality: Qi, precision, nerve and pressure-point targeting

The Snake is the most internal of the five. Snake techniques target the nervous system: finger-tip strikes to pressure points, tendons, and nerve clusters. The strike is not powerful by mass or speed alone — it is precise. The physics here is pressure at a point: the same force delivered to one square millimetre versus one square centimetre produces vastly different results. Snake practice also develops exceptional qi cultivation through its breathing patterns and spinal undulation.

  • Pressure point targeting
  • Finger-tip and spear-hand strikes
  • Qi cultivation and breath control
鹤

Crane — 鹤形

Hè Xíng

Core quality: Balance, grace, evasion, deflection

The Crane stands on one leg in the storm. Crane Fist trains extraordinary balance and the ability to redirect force without absorbing it. Crane wing strikes deflect and strike simultaneously. One-legged stances develop the deep hip stabilisers and the proprioceptive systems that underpin all martial skill. The Crane also teaches patience — waiting for the precise moment, then striking with surgical accuracy.

  • Single-leg balance and stability
  • Wing-arm deflections and strikes
  • Evasion and redirection

太极拳

Shaolin Tai Chi 太极拳

Tai Chi Chuan (太极拳 tàijí quán — "Supreme Ultimate Fist") is the internal martial art that emerged from Shaolin principles. Where external Shaolin forms develop power and speed, Tai Chi develops sensitivity, yielding, and the ability to use an opponent's force against them. Both arts are the same physics — Tai Chi just makes it slow enough to feel.

初级
二十四式太极拳 Èrshísì Shì Tàijí Quán

24-Form Tai Chi

The simplified 24-form is the standard entry point for Tai Chi practice worldwide, developed in 1956 to distil the essence of Yang-style Tai Chi into an accessible sequence. Do not be deceived by "simplified" — the form teaches every fundamental principle of Tai Chi: continuous circular movement, weight shifting, silk-reeling (缠丝 chán sī), and the unity of upper and lower body. All 24 postures have direct martial applications; learning them slowly is the point, not a compromise.

Weight ShiftingSilk ReelingContinuous FlowRooting
中级
四十八式太极拳 Sìshíbā Shì Tàijí Quán

48-Form Combined Tai Chi

The 48-form draws from multiple Tai Chi families — Yang, Chen, Wu and Sun — creating a comprehensive practice that exposes the student to different expressions of the same underlying principles. Chen-style techniques introduce fa jing (发劲) — the explosive release of stored elastic energy — which reveals that Tai Chi is not inherently soft; softness is the preparation for sudden hardness. This is advanced physics: elastic potential energy stored in tendons and fascia, released in a fraction of a second.

Fa JingMulti-StyleElastic PowerExplosive Release
中级
少林太极拳 Shàolín Tàijí Quán

Shaolin Tai Chi

The Shaolin Temple's own Tai Chi form integrates the circular, yielding principles of Tai Chi with the structural conditioning of Shaolin external practice. Transitions between techniques are deliberately ambiguous — a deflection can become a strike; a step backward can generate a forward throw. This form is where external and internal practice converge, and where students begin to understand that the distinction between "hard" and "soft" styles is a teaching fiction, not a physical reality.

External-Internal BridgeApplicationsTransition Mastery
高级
推手 Tuī Shǒu

Push Hands

Push Hands is not a form but the partner practice that tests Tai Chi principles under live pressure. Two practitioners maintain contact at the wrists and forearms, each attempting to uproot, redirect, or unbalance the other while remaining rooted themselves. It teaches listening (听劲 tīng jìn) — feeling the opponent's weight, intention, and force vector through touch — and the ability to respond with minimum effort. This is Newton's Third Law as a martial art: every force has an equal and opposite reaction; the one who finds the path of least resistance wins.

Partner PracticeListening JinUprootingSensitivity

兵器

Traditional Weapons 兵器

Shaolin weapons training (兵器 bīng qì) is not an addition to the curriculum — it is an extension of the body. Each weapon has a different length, weight, and balance point, which forces the body to adapt its mechanics. Weapons training reveals weaknesses in bare-hand practice and deepens understanding of range, leverage, and force application. In Shaolin tradition, weapons are taught only after a solid foundation of bare-hand forms is established.

"手是两扇门，全凭脚打人" — The hands are two gates; all power comes from the legs. This is even more true with a weapon.

棍

Staff — 棍

Gùn

The staff is the first weapon taught at Red Dragons and in the Shaolin tradition — because it is the most honest. A six-foot hardwood staff has no edge, no point, and no mechanical advantage other than leverage. Everything it does, your body must do. Staff training develops two-handed coordination, full-body rotation for power transfer, and an instinctive understanding of lever mechanics. The primary tao lu we teach:

  • 少林齐眉棍 Shàolín Qí Méi Gùn Shaolin Eyebrow-Level Staff
  • 少林大棍 Shàolín Dà Gùn Shaolin Long Staff
Physics principle: Lever arm and rotational momentum — the same force applied further from the pivot produces greater speed at the tip.
刀

Broadsword — 刀

Dāo

The dao (broadsword or sabre) is single-edged, curved, and designed for sweeping, slashing cuts. It is a weapon of continuous flow — the momentum of one cut is recycled into the next, meaning the student must learn circular mechanics and never let the blade die. The dao is described in Chinese martial arts as having a "fierce and brave" character (勇猛). Broadsword training develops dynamic hip rotation, single-arm coordination, and the ability to change direction while maintaining blade control.

  • 少林单刀 Shàolín Dān Dāo Shaolin Single Broadsword
  • 少林双刀 Shàolín Shuāng Dāo Shaolin Double Broadsword
Physics principle: Centripetal force and momentum conservation — keeping the blade moving in arcs requires less energy than stopping and restarting.
剑

Straight Sword — 剑

Jiàn

The jian (straight double-edged sword) is considered the "gentleman of weapons" (君子剑 jūnzǐ jiàn) in Chinese martial arts. Where the dao is powerful and circular, the jian is refined and precise. It uses the wrist and fingers to direct the blade — making it the weapon that most demands internal power and sensitivity. A jian practitioner must be able to distinguish between the flat, the edge, and the point and use all three tactically. This is a surgeon's weapon: every technique is specific, economical, and deliberate.

  • 少林单剑 Shàolín Dān Jiàn Shaolin Single Straight Sword
  • 少林太极剑 Shàolín Tàijí Jiàn Shaolin Tai Chi Sword
Physics principle: Point of application — the tip moves fastest and delivers the most penetrating energy; wrist micro-adjustments control direction at range.
枪

Spear — 枪

Qiāng

The spear is called the "king of long weapons" (百兵之王 bǎi bīng zhī wáng) in Chinese martial arts. Its reach makes it dominant in open space; its thrusting speed — generated by a whipping action along the full length of the shaft — is almost impossible to parry. Shaolin spear work develops the full extension of power from the rear foot, through the hips and torso, along both arms, to the tip. It makes visible what is hidden in bare-hand technique.

  • 少林花枪 Shàolín Huā Qiāng Shaolin Flower Spear
  • 少林六合枪 Shàolín Liùhé Qiāng Shaolin Six Harmony Spear
Physics principle: Wave propagation — the whipping motion of the shaft travels as a mechanical wave from grip to tip, multiplying the speed of thrust.
鞭

Nine-Section Whip Chain — 九节鞭

Jiǔ Jié Biān

The nine-section whip chain is the most challenging weapon in the Shaolin arsenal and one of the most spectacular. Nine metal rings joined by short chains, with a dart at each end. It is entirely flexible and must be controlled entirely through body movement and centripetal force — there is no rigid structure to rely on. Training it requires mastery of all the principles from every other weapon and form, which is why it is reserved for advanced students. It is the ultimate test of whether the physics is truly internal.

  • 九节鞭 Jiǔ Jié Biān Nine-Section Whip Form
Physics principle: Centripetal force and angular momentum — once the chain is moving, the physics keeps it moving; the practitioner's job is to guide and redirect, not power it.
棒

Three-Section Staff — 三节棍

Sān Jié Gùn

Three hardwood sections joined by short chains. The three-section staff combines the reach of the staff with the unpredictability of a flexible weapon. It can be used as a rigid staff when sections are locked by grip, or as a whipping weapon when momentum is transferred through the joints. This duality — rigid when needed, flexible when not — mirrors the highest Shaolin principle: hard and soft, in the right proportion, at the right moment.

  • 少林三节棍 Shàolín Sān Jié Gùn Shaolin Three-Section Staff Form
Physics principle: Rigid-body vs. flexible dynamics — the same weapon behaves under completely different mechanical models depending on how it is gripped and moved.
